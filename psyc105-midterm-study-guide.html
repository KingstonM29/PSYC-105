Chapter 8 · Thinking and Language

What is special about language?

Language has structural features (the building blocks) and properties (what makes it different from other communication).

Structural features

FeatureWhat it isExample
PhonemesThe smallest units of sound in a language/ch/ /ai/ /r/ /s/
MorphemesThe smallest units of meaning (whole words, prefixes, suffixes)(chair)(s): “chair” + plural “-s”
SyntaxThe rules for ordering words into sentences“Dog bites man” vs. “Man bites dog”
Extralinguistic informationMeaning that comes from outside the words: tone of voice, facial expression, gestures, context“Great job…” said sarcastically
Try it

Break a word into phonemes and morphemes

Phonemes count sounds, not letters. Morphemes count meaningful pieces.

Properties of language

Generative

A limited set of sounds and words can be combined into an unlimited number of new sentences that others understand.

Arbitrary / symbolic

Words are symbols. “Cat” doesn't sound or look like a cat; the link between word and meaning is agreed on, not natural.

Specialized brain areas

Language relies on dedicated regions, usually in the left hemisphere (e.g., Broca's area for producing speech, Wernicke's area for understanding it).

Developmental stages

Children acquire language in a predictable sequence. Deaf babies show manual babbling (repetitive hand shapes, around 9 months), just as hearing babies babble sounds.

Class activity · gesture game

Why was guessing the signed sentences so hard?

We made up gestures and tried to guess each other's sentences. It was difficult because it's hard to invent a sign for many words, and we don't already know the correct ASL sign for each word.

Key takeaway

True sign languages are real languages

  • They have the same structural features as spoken languages (units, meaning pieces, syntax).
  • Signs are generally arbitrary and symbolic, not just pantomime, which is why you can't guess them.
  • They follow the same developmental stages (manual babbling).
Chapter 8 · Measuring Intelligence

The WAIS-IV and test bias

The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS-IV) gives an overall IQ built from four index scores. Each index is measured by specific subtests.

Verbal Comprehension

  • Similarities · how are two things alike?
  • Vocabulary · define words
  • Information · general-knowledge questions

Perceptual Reasoning

  • Block Design · copy a pattern with coloured blocks
  • Matrix Reasoning · pick the piece that completes a pattern
  • Visual Puzzles · pick pieces that build a shape

Working Memory

  • Digit Span · repeat strings of numbers
  • Arithmetic · solve math problems in your head

Processing Speed

  • Symbol Search · spot target symbols quickly
  • Coding · copy symbols paired with numbers, against the clock

Memory hook: Verbal, Perceptual, Working memory, Speed. Two quadrants have 3 subtests (top row), two have 2 (bottom row).

Class activity · the 10-question “IQ test”

What each question measured, and where bias crept in

Questions were read only once, under time pressure. Afterward, groups decided which skill each item tested and whether it was biased.

QItem (roughly)Skill measuredBias?
1Letter task (A B C D … P), remembered from one readingWorking memory
2Copy symbols using a number-to-symbol keyProcessing speed
3How many legs: Deacon, Pickerel, Manx, Weimaraner, ChesterfieldVerbal / vocabularyBiased not common words
4Mental math problemArithmetic / working memory
5Cross out shapes/numbers following spoken rulesPerceptual / working memory
6Fill in numbers in a seriesWorking memory

Sources of unfairness: unfamiliar vocabulary or cultural knowledge, ambiguous wording or figures, and time pressure. A test can end up measuring background and test conditions instead of ability.

History · 1917

Army Beta IQ test

A picture-based test given to WWI U.S. Army recruits who couldn't read English. Test 6 asked recruits to spot the missing part of each drawing: a bowling alley, tennis court, phonograph, playing card. You can only answer if you grew up with those objects. A non-verbal test is not automatically culture-free.

Discussion

What should intelligence tests measure?

  • Most modern IQ tests focus on cognitive abilities (like the four WAIS-IV indexes).
  • Some researchers argue other factors should be included, such as creativity, practical/everyday problem solving, or emotional skills. Check your textbook for the theories your course names.
Chapter 11 · Stress

Three ways to define and measure stress

Each approach measures something different, and each of the first two has a major issue that the next one fixes. Know the measure and the limitation for each.

1 · Stress as a stimulus

Stressors: the things that stress us out

Measures:

  • Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS): 43 major life events with point values (death of spouse 100, divorce 73, marriage 50, fired at work 47, retirement 45).
  • Daily Hassles Scale: everyday annoyances rated 0 / 5 / 10 (missing the bus, too little money, noisy housemate, laundry).
Major issue: counting the events that happen to people doesn't tell us how stressed they felt about them.
2 · Stress as a response

How we react: thoughts, feelings, behaviours, physical sensations

Stress mobilizes energy for fight or flight.

Measures (physiological):

  • Heart rate
  • Blood pressure
  • Galvanic skin response (sweat / skin conductance)
  • Hormones, especially cortisol (blood or saliva)
Major issue: body changes can have causes other than stress (e.g., excitement), and they don't tell us how the person is interpreting the sensations.
3 · Stress as a transaction

Event + how you appraise it

Stimulus+Appraisal=Response

Event + “How well can I handle it?” = how stressed you feel.

Stress happens when demands outweigh resources.

Measure: Perceived Stress Scale (PSS): 10 items about the last month, rated 0 (never) to 4 (very often). E.g., “How often have you felt unable to control the important things in your life?”

Chapter 11 · Appraisal and Coping

From event to coping

Example: your car breaks down
E

Event

Something happens.

1

Primary appraisal

Is this a threat? What are the demands?
No → carry on, no stress. Yes → go to secondary appraisal.

2

Secondary appraisal

How well can I cope? What are my resources?
Minimal resources → STRESS. Many resources → less stress.

C

Coping

Change the situation, change your reaction, or prevent it next time.

Problem-focused

Change the SITUATION

Call a tow truck.

Emotion-focused

Change your REACTION

Deep breathing, shifting your focus, cognitive reappraisal (thinking about it differently).

Proactive

PREVENT future stressors

Schedule regular maintenance on your car.

If you can't change the situation, emotion-focused coping helps right now. If you want a different outcome, you need problem-focused coping. Big, complicated stressors usually need both.

Class activity · scenarios

The three in-class scenarios, with the answer key

Scenario 1: Surprise quiz you forgot about (worth 5%)

You arrive in class and discover a scheduled quiz you didn't put in your calendar, so you haven't studied.

Type: Daily hassle. Group stress ratings (1–5): 2, 5, 4, 3, showing that people appraise the same event differently.

  • Problem Quickly look over your notes
  • Emotion Try to relax because it's not worth that much
  • Problem Lie to your prof about being sick to get out of it (changes the situation, though not a good idea)
  • Proactive Think about how to prevent this next time (calendar)

Which coping? Could be either, but you can't change the quiz, so emotion-focused coping helps most right now.

Scenario 2: Best friend's party vs. a shift you can't skip

Your best friend's birthday party is next Friday, but you're scheduled to work. Skipping work means not making rent.

Type: Daily hassle. Ratings: 3, 1, 4, 3.

  • Problem Try to find someone to cover your shift
  • Emotion Realize that your friend will understand
  • Problem Try to get the party moved

Which coping? Could be either, but if you really want to go, problem-focused coping is necessary.

Scenario 3: New job in a new city in three weeks

You need to move (packing, cleaning, finding a place), buy work clothes, and wrap up loose ends before you go.

Type: Major life event (or two!). Ratings: 4, 5, 4, 3.

  • Problem Make a list and prioritize things
  • Emotion Focus on the great opportunities and how exciting it is
  • Problem Ask friends and family for help with moving
  • Problem Try to get enough sleep and good food

Which coping? You'll need both: lots to do, and you also need to adjust your thinking and emotions.

Individual differences

Same event, different stress

  • People perceive different amounts of stress for the same situation.
  • That changes wellbeing: some feel fine at exam time, or excited rather than terrified by big life changes.
  • To reduce stress: reappraise the situation (emotion-focused) and be proactive (organized and prepared, so you're ready for more).
  • Downside of low stress: stress helps us focus on important tasks. Without it, you might not change or improve.
Emotion-focused technique

Diaphragmatic breathing

Activates the parasympathetic nervous system via the vagus nerve. It changes your reaction to stress.

Ready
  1. Sit with both feet flat on the floor.
  2. One hand on your chest, one on your belly below the ribs.
  3. Inhale for 3: belly moves out, chest and shoulders stay still.
  4. Exhale for 5 in a slow, steady stream.
  5. Focus on the breathing, not the stressful thoughts.

Chapter 11 · Measuring stress

Three approaches to stress

Describe the three approaches to defining stress and give one way each is measured. Then explain one limitation of the stimulus approach and one limitation of the response approach.

3 approaches with a measure 1 each = 3 · limitations 1 + 1 = 5
Answer key
  • 1Stimulus: stress is the event (stressor). Measured with the SRRS (major life events) or Daily Hassles Scale.
  • 1Response: stress is how we react (thoughts, feelings, behaviours, physical sensations). Measured with heart rate, blood pressure, galvanic skin response, or cortisol.
  • 1Transaction: stimulus + appraisal = response; stress when demands exceed resources. Measured with the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS).
  • 1Stimulus limitation: knowing what happened doesn't tell you how stressed the person felt.
  • 1Response limitation: physical changes could come from something other than stress (e.g., excitement), and don't show how the person interprets them.
Chapter 11 · Appraisal and coping

Laptop crash

Your laptop crashes the night before a paper is due. Describe what happens during primary and secondary appraisal in this situation. Then give one problem-focused, one emotion-focused, and one proactive coping strategy.

Appraisals 1 + 1 · coping strategies 1 + 1 + 1 = 5
Answer key
  • 1Primary: deciding whether it's a threat and what it demands. Yes: the paper is due and the file may be lost.
  • 1Secondary: judging how well you can cope with your resources (a backup copy, a library computer, an understanding prof). Few resources → more stress.
  • 1Problem-focused (change the situation): use a library computer, recover the file, ask for an extension.
  • 1Emotion-focused (change your reaction): diaphragmatic breathing, reappraise (“one paper won't decide my degree”).
  • 1Proactive (prevent future stressors): set up automatic cloud backups, finish papers earlier.
